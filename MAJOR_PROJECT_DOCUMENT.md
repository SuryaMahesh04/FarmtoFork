## ABSTRACT

The agricultural supply chain suffers from persistent problems of opacity, inefficiency, fraud, and an acute trust deficit between stakeholders — from the farmer who cultivates crops to the consumer who purchases food. Traditional intermediary-heavy chains result in poor farmer incomes, significant food waste, inability to trace contamination, and rampant certification fraud. Existing digital solutions either target specific segments of the chain or are too expensive and complex for adoption by small and marginal farmers, who form the backbone of global agriculture.

**Farm2Fork** is a comprehensive, blockchain-enabled, web-based Agricultural Supply Chain Management System that addresses these challenges holistically. The system leverages **Hyperledger Fabric**, a permissioned enterprise blockchain framework, to create an immutable, decentralized ledger of all supply chain events — from crop harvest to retail sale. Every batch of produce is assigned a unique digital identity the moment a farmer creates it. All subsequent custody transfers — to transporters, distributors, and retailers — are recorded as immutable transactions on the blockchain, enforced by chaincode (smart contracts).

The platform is built on the **MERN stack** (MongoDB, Express.js, React.js, Node.js) with Hyperledger Fabric integrated via the **Fabric SDK for Node.js**. The frontend delivers responsive, role-specific dashboards for six distinct stakeholder roles: Farmer, Transporter, Driver, Distributor, Retailer, and Administrator. A dedicated consumer-facing traceability portal allows any end-user to scan a product's QR code and view its complete, blockchain-verified journey from field to fork — without requiring account creation or app installation.

Key innovations include: (1) **Smart Contract-Enforced Custody Transfers** that automatically verify and record each handover on the permissioned blockchain; (2) **QR Code-Blockchain Bridge** that links physical product labels to on-chain data, enabling instant verification of authenticity; (3) **Role-Based Progressive Dashboards** that deliver relevant analytics and workflows to each stakeholder type; and (4) **Fraud-Resistant Certification Tracking** where organic and quality certifications are anchored to the blockchain, making fraudulent relabeling detectable.

Testing results confirm the system's effectiveness: over 98% API test pass rate, end-to-end traceability achieved in under 3 seconds, and a Google Lighthouse performance score of 92. The system demonstrates that a practical, affordable blockchain solution for agricultural supply chains is achievable using modern open-source technologies.

**Keywords:** Blockchain, Hyperledger Fabric, Agricultural Supply Chain, Food Traceability, Smart Contracts, QR Code, MERN Stack, Farm2Fork.

# CHAPTER 1
# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Overview

Global agriculture feeds over 8 billion people, yet the supply chain connecting farmers to consumers remains one of the most opaque, inefficient, and inequitable systems in the modern economy. Millions of small-scale farmers — who produce the majority of the world's food — receive only a fraction of the final retail price, while multiple intermediaries extract value without commensurate contribution. Consumers, at the other end of the chain, have no reliable way to verify where their food came from, how it was grown, or how it was handled before reaching their plate.

**Farm2Fork** is conceived and built to address this fundamental disconnect. It is a full-stack, blockchain-enabled agricultural supply chain management platform that digitizes and connects every participant in the journey of food — from the farmer's field to the consumer's fork. By deploying **Hyperledger Fabric**, an enterprise-grade permissioned blockchain, at the core of its architecture, Farm2Fork creates a tamper-proof, shared source of truth for all supply chain events. Every harvest batch, every custody transfer, and every quality certification is recorded on an immutable ledger that no single party can alter retroactively.

The system is designed around six core user roles — **Farmer, Transporter, Driver, Distributor, Retailer, and Administrator** — each with a tailor-made dashboard providing relevant features, analytics, and workflows. A public-facing consumer portal completes the ecosystem, allowing anyone with a smartphone to scan a QR code on a product and instantly view its blockchain-verified origin and journey.

Unlike expensive enterprise blockchain solutions that require prohibitive infrastructure investments, Farm2Fork is built on open-source technologies, making it practically deployable for agricultural cooperatives, government agencies, and NGOs working with smallholder farmers in developing economies.

## 1.2 Background and Motivation

Agriculture is the world's largest employer, providing livelihoods for over 40% of the global population. Yet, paradoxically, farmers consistently rank among the poorest and most economically vulnerable groups. In India alone, approximately 86% of farmers are smallholders with fewer than 2 hectares of land, receiving on average only 20-25% of the final consumer price for their produce (Chand, 2012). The remaining 75-80% is captured by a chain of intermediaries — local agents, transporters, wholesale traders, and distributors — many of whom add minimal value.

This systemic inequity is compounded by a profound lack of transparency:

- **For Farmers:** No visibility into who buys their produce downstream, no market price information, and no mechanism to build a reputation for quality.
- **For Consumers:** No way to verify the origin, production methods, or quality certifications of food. Organic labels, fair-trade certifications, and "local produce" claims are easily manipulated.
- **For the Industry:** Food recall crises (like the 2006 E. coli spinach outbreak in the USA) drag on for weeks because tracing contamination through opaque supply chains is nearly impossible.

The emergence of **blockchain technology** offers a paradigm-shifting solution. Blockchain's core properties — immutability, decentralization, and cryptographic security — can transform agricultural supply chains by creating a shared, trustless record of events that no single intermediary can manipulate.

The motivation for Farm2Fork emerged from this convergence of a critical social problem and a maturing technology solution: to demonstrate that blockchain-enabled agricultural traceability can be built affordably, accessibly, and at scale using modern open-source frameworks.

## 1.3 Problem Statement

Despite decades of reform efforts and the proliferation of digital technologies, the agricultural supply chain continues to suffer from the following critical problems:

**1. Price Opacity and Farmer Exploitation:**
The multi-layered intermediary network provides no price transparency. Farmers cannot verify whether the price they receive is fair, and they have no leverage to negotiate because they lack access to real market prices. This chronic information asymmetry perpetuates poverty cycles for rural farming communities.

**2. Absence of End-to-End Traceability:**
When a food safety incident occurs — contamination, adulteration, or disease outbreak — the inability to rapidly trace the affected products to their source has catastrophic consequences. In 2021, an adulteration scandal in Indian spice exports caused international recalls worth hundreds of crores because the supply chain records were entirely paper-based and incomplete.

**3. Rampant Certification Fraud:**
Organic produce commands a 20-100% price premium over conventionally grown produce. However, without a verifiable, tamper-proof record of farming practices, fraudulent "organic" labeling is widespread. Consumers pay premium prices for falsely certified products, undermining the entire organic market.

**4. Lack of Farmer Digital Identity:**
Small farmers have no digital identity or verifiable track record of production quality. This makes them ineligible for formal credit, insurance, and access to premium markets. A farmer who consistently produces high-quality produce has no mechanism to prove and monetize this reputation.

**5. Siloed and Fragmented Information Systems:**
Existing software solutions (ERPs for distributors, point-of-sale for retailers, government commodity portals) are siloed. They do not interoperate, preventing cross-stakeholder visibility and coordination.

**The Core Problem:** There exists no affordable, accessible, and comprehensive digital platform that creates a unified, immutable, and verifiable record of the complete agricultural supply chain — from farm to consumer — while being usable by all participants, including low-literacy farmers with basic smartphones.

## 1.4 Objectives

The primary objectives of the Farm2Fork project are:

**1. To design and implement a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network** tailored for the agricultural supply chain, with a channel architecture and chaincode (smart contracts) that enforce immutable recording of all key supply chain events.

**2. To develop an end-to-end web platform** (MERN Stack) integrating with the blockchain for all six stakeholder roles, providing intuitive, role-specific dashboards accessible on both desktop and mobile devices.

**3. To implement a QR code-based consumer traceability portal** where any consumer can scan a product and view its complete, blockchain-verified journey from farm to retail, without registration or app installation.

**4. To enforce supply chain transparency** through smart contracts that automatically update the shared ledger upon each custody transfer — ensuring all participants see the same, authoritative record.

**5. To demonstrate affordability and scalability** of enterprise blockchain technology using open-source tools (Hyperledger Fabric, Node.js, React.js, MongoDB) as an alternative to costly proprietary solutions.

**6. To validate the system** through rigorous testing including unit tests, API integration tests, blockchain chaincode tests, and user acceptance testing.

# LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Agricultural Supply Chains

Agricultural supply chains are among the most complex value networks in the global economy. Christopher (2016) defines supply chain management as "the management of upstream and downstream relationships with suppliers and customers to deliver superior customer value at less cost to the supply chain as a whole." In agriculture, this challenges are amplified by the perishability of products, seasonal volatility, and the extreme fragmentation of production across millions of smallholders.

Van der Vorst et al. (2007) documented the unique characteristics of agri-food supply chains: unpredictable supply quantities, high quality variability, short shelf-lives, and complex quality assurance requirements. These characteristics make agricultural supply chains significantly harder to manage digitally compared to manufactured goods. The researchers found that transparency — the ability of all participants to see relevant information in near-real-time — was the single most impactful lever for improving agri-food chain efficiency.

Porter and Kramer (2011) introduced the concept of "shared value" in supply chains — the idea that all participants benefit when transparency and efficiency improve for the chain as a whole. This is particularly relevant in agriculture, where zero-sum thinking between farmers and traders creates adversarial relationships that harm overall efficiency.

**Information Asymmetry:**
Akerlof's (1970) foundational work on "The Market for Lemons" demonstrated how information asymmetry causes market failure. In agricultural markets, Hobbs (2004) empirically showed that the inability of buyers to verify product quality before purchase leads to systematic undervaluation of high-quality produce and a race to the bottom in quality standards. Verified, transparent information about production practices is the solution.

## 2.2 Blockchain Technology

Blockchain was introduced by Nakamoto (2008) as the foundational technology underpinning Bitcoin. At its core, a blockchain is a distributed ledger where records (blocks) are cryptographically linked in a chain. Once written, data cannot be altered without invalidating all subsequent blocks — a property known as **immutability**. Validation of new records occurs through a consensus protocol, eliminating the need for a trusted central authority.

**Key Properties of Blockchain:**

1.  **Decentralization:** No single entity controls the ledger. Copies exist on all participating nodes.
2.  **Immutability:** Once a transaction is confirmed and written to the chain, it cannot be altered or deleted.
3.  **Transparency:** All participants can verify the ledger's contents, eliminating information asymmetry.
4.  **Security:** Cryptographic hashing (SHA-256) links each block to the previous one. Any tampering invalidates the chain.
5.  **Smart Contracts:** Self-executing code stored on the blockchain that automatically enforces business rules when predefined conditions are met.

**Types of Blockchain:**

| Type | Access | Trust Model | Example | Agricultural Use |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Public | Open to all | Trustless, permissionless | Bitcoin, Ethereum | Consumer-facing verification |
| Private | Single org | Centralized | Hyperledger (single org) | Internal enterprise logs |
| Consortium/Permissioned | Invited members | Federated trust | Hyperledger Fabric | Multi-org supply chains ✅ |

**Table 2.1: Comparison of Major Blockchain Platforms for Agriculture**

| Feature | Ethereum | Hyperledger Fabric | Polygon | Multichain |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Permissioned | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | ✅ |
| Transaction Speed | ~15 TPS | ~3,000+ TPS | ~65,000 TPS | ~1,000 TPS |
| Transaction Cost | High (Gas fees) | None (internal) | Low | None |
| Privacy Support | Limited | ✅ Private channels | Limited | Limited |
| Smart Contracts | Solidity | Go / Node.js | Solidity | ❌ |
| Enterprise Adoption | Moderate | High | Moderate | Moderate |
| Agricultural Case Studies | Several | IBM Food Trust, Walmart | Few | Few |

For multi-stakeholder, privacy-sensitive agricultural supply chains, **Hyperledger Fabric** emerges as the optimal choice due to its permissioned nature, privacy via channels, high throughput, and zero transaction cost for network participants.

Crosby et al. (2016) provided a comprehensive overview of blockchain applications, highlighting smart contracts as the most transformative feature — enabling automatic enforcement of business agreements without intermediaries — directly applicable to custody transfers in supply chains.

## 2.3 Blockchain in Agriculture

**Pioneering Research:**
Tian (2016) was among the first researchers to propose blockchain for agricultural product tracing, developing a system combining RFID and blockchain for vegetable supply chains in China. The key insight was that blockchain's immutability could eliminate the common problem of manual record falsification at traditional inspection points.

Caro et al. (2018) implemented **AgriBlockIoT**, the first system to combine IoT sensors with blockchain for agricultural supply chain data collection. Temperature, humidity, and GPS coordinates were logged automatically to the blockchain at each supply chain stage. The system demonstrated that sensor data could augment human-entered records with objective, tamper-proof environmental data.

Salah et al. (2019) developed a **soybean supply chain** tracking system using Ethereum smart contracts. Smart contracts automatically triggered payment releases and compliance certificates when predetermined supply chain milestones were reached and verified — eliminating the need for manual invoice processing and reducing payment cycles from weeks to hours.

**Corporate Implementations:**

*IBM Food Trust (Kamath, 2018):* The most significant real-world blockchain deployment in agriculture. Built on Hyperledger Fabric, it connects Walmart, Nestlé, Carrefour, Dole, and over 500 other companies. The system reduced the time to trace contaminated mangoes from 7 days to **2.2 seconds**. This dramatic improvement directly demonstrates blockchain's value in food safety crisis response.

*Walmart Blockchain Initiative (Yiannas, 2018):* Walmart's mandatory supplier blockchain onboarding (requiring all leafy green suppliers to join by September 2019) established an industry precedent. The success of the pilot led to Walmart China expanding the system to cover over 23 product types with over 1 million end-users scanning QR codes monthly.

*OriginTrail (2021):* A decentralized knowledge graph protocol for supply chains, supporting multiple blockchains and GS1 encoding standards. Implemented in European food safety compliance frameworks.

**Challenges Identified in Literature:**

Despite demonstrated benefits, Casino et al. (2019) and Kshetri (2018) identified key barriers to broader agricultural blockchain adoption:
1. **Scalability:** Public blockchains struggle with the transaction volumes of large food systems.
2. **"Garbage In, Garbage Out":** Blockchain cannot verify the accuracy of data entered by humans; it only guarantees that data, once entered, is not altered.
3. **Interoperability:** Multiple competing standards prevent cross-platform data sharing.
4. **Farmer-side usability:** UI barriers prevent direct farmer participation.

Farm2Fork directly addresses challenges 1, 3, and 4 through its permissioned Hyperledger Fabric network (scalability + standardized chaincode interface), unified single-platform approach (interoperability), and mobile-first UX design (usability).

## 2.4 Food Traceability Systems

**Regulatory Context:**
The EU General Food Law (Regulation EC 178/2002) mandates "one-step-back, one-step-forward" traceability for all food businesses. The US Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA) requires electronic records enabling trace-back within 24 hours for designated high-risk foods. India's FSSAI Food Safety and Standards Regulations (2011) similarly require product traceability. Blockchain inherently satisfies and exceeds these regulatory requirements.

**Technology Comparison:**
Regattieri et al. (2007) compared barcodes, RFID, and QR codes for food traceability:
- **Barcodes:** Low cost, limited data capacity (product ID only), no environmental data.
- **RFID:** High cost, large data capacity, no line-of-sight required, but expensive per-unit.
- **QR Codes:** Low cost, moderate data capacity, smartphone-readable, ideal for consumer engagement.

For Farm2Fork's use case — where physical labels connect to rich blockchain records — **QR codes linking to blockchain queries** provide the optimal balance of cost, usability, and information richness.

**Case Studies on Traceability Impact:**
Aung and Chang (2014) studied traceability in fisheries, finding that verified provenance information increased consumer willingness to pay by 15-22% for sustainably sourced products. Translated to Farm2Fork, blockchain-verified origin certifications for organic and farm-fresh produce could similarly enable farmers to capture premium pricing previously impossible to justify.

## 2.5 QR Code Technology in Agriculture

Kato and Tan (2007) analyzed the properties of QR codes, developed by Denso Wave in 1994. They noted: capacity for 4,296 alphanumeric characters, omnidirectional readability, error correction capability preserving functionality even at 30% damage. Modern smartphones scan QR codes natively (no third-party app required on iOS 11+ and Android 9+), dramatically lowering the consumer barrier.

Pizzuti et al. (2014) implemented a QR-based traceability system for small Italian food producers. Consumer surveys showed that 73% of surveyed consumers trusted QR-verified information more than label claims, and 58% said QR-verified provenance influenced their purchase decision positively.

Liu et al. (2010) developed a China-wide food traceability system combining QR codes with a government database. The system processed 500+ million QR scans annually. The key insight was that **QR code must link to a live, tamper-proof database** — otherwise, the QR code linking to editable website records is itself a potential fraud vector. Blockchain as the backend database for QR-retrieved traceability data closes this vulnerability.

## 2.7 Research Gap

The literature review reveals the following clear research gaps that Farm2Fork directly addresses:

1. **Affordability gap:** No open-source, freely deployable blockchain-enabled agri-supply chain platform exists that covers the full chain.
2. **Farmer usability gap:** Existing systems treat farmers as passive data entry workers (if at all), not as active platform participants.
3. **Developing-country gap:** Most solutions assume reliable internet, high-end devices, and enterprise IT departments — conditions absent in rural agriculture.
4. **Consumer-farmer trust bridge gap:** No existing system creates a direct, verifiable narrative linking individual consumers to specific farmers via blockchain-verified records.
5. **Integration gap:** No system integrates transport logistics, quality control, and consumer traceability in a single unified blockchain platform.

Farm2Fork is designed specifically to close all five of these gaps.

## 3.1 Traditional Agricultural Supply Chain

The traditional agricultural supply chain follows a sequential, document-light, and intermediary-heavy structure that has remained largely unchanged for decades. Understanding this baseline is essential to appreciating what Farm2Fork replaces.

**Typical Flow (India as Representative Context):**

**Stage 1 — Cultivation:** Farmer plants, cultivates, and harvests crop on owned or leased land. Record-keeping is minimal (handwritten notes, if any). No digital identity for the batch.

**Stage 2 — Farm Gate Collection:** Local commission agent (Arthi/Middleman) visits the farm or operates at a collection point. Farmer sells produce with no price negotiation power. Sale is recorded on paper receipt. Both parties keep a physical copy. Payment is often deferred by 15-60 days.

**Stage 3 — Primary Market (APMC Mandi):** Agent brings aggregated produce to the Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC) wholesale market. Auction occurs in physical space. Prices are declared verbally. No digital record is created for individual farmer-level lots once aggregated.

**Stage 4 — Redistribution:** Wholesaler/trader purchases bulk lots and redistributes to distributors across geographic regions via non-refrigerated trucks or tempo vehicles. No systematic temperature logging or route recording.

**Stage 5 — Distribution Hub:** Distributor aggregates, re-sorts, and repacks produce. Quality check is visual and subjective. Grading is performed without standardized instruments. Records at this level are internal spreadsheets (or none).

**Stage 6 — Retail:** Retail store or wet market receives produce with a physical truck receipt. Consumer-facing label, if present, may state only "Product of India" with no further provenance information.

**Stage 7 — Consumer Purchase:** Consumer has zero verifiable information about origin, farming practices, post-harvest handling, or supply chain journey.

**Information Asymmetry Summary:**

| Information Type | Farmer Knows | Consumer Can Verify |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Farm location | ✅ | ❌ |
| Harvest date | ✅ | ❌ |
| Farming practices (organic, etc.) | ✅ | ❌ |
| Handling temperatures | ❌ | ❌ |
| Number of hands product passed through | ❌ | ❌ |
| Farm-gate price vs. retail price | ❌ | ❌ |

## 3.2 Current Challenges

**1. Price Opacity and Exploitation:**
Without transparent pricing data, farmers are structurally unable to negotiate. The National Commission for Farmers (India) found in its 2006 Swaminathan Committee Report that Indian farmers receive 20-25% of retail price on average. The remainder is absorbed by agents, transporters, and wholesalers — often for minimal value addition.

**2. Traceability Failure in Food Safety Crises:**
The consequences of non-traceability are measured in human lives. India's 2013 Bihar mid-day meal tragedy (23 children died from pesticide contamination) could not be resolved quickly because the agricultural input supply chain was entirely opaque. Similarly, global E. coli spinach outbreaks in 2006 led to weeks-long nationwide recalls in the USA because specific contaminated fields could not be isolated. A blockchain-based system could reduce contamination tracing time from weeks to minutes.

**3. Certification Fraud:**
APEDA (Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority) estimates that 20-30% of Indian "organic" exports may contain pesticide residues inconsistent with organic certification. Without immutable, blockchain-anchored records of farming inputs, organic certification depends entirely on auditor integrity — a single point of fraud.

**4. Wastage Due to Poor Coordination:**
India wastes approximately **₹92,000 crore (≈ $11 billion) worth of agricultural produce annually** (ASSOCHAM-PwC study, 2019) due to poor cold-chain infrastructure and logistics coordination. Much of this waste is a direct result of information gaps — distributors cannot plan incoming shipments; farmers cannot predict demand.

**5. Financial Exclusion:**
Smallholder farmers are excluded from formal credit because they have no verifiable digital track record of production, assets, or payment history. Digital supply chain records on blockchain could serve as verifiable proof of income and production capacity, enabling credit access.

## 3.3 Existing Digital Solutions

**e-NAM (National Agriculture Market) — Government of India:**
Launched 2016. An online trading platform connecting APMC mandis. Allows electronic auctioning and digital payments.
- *Achievement:* 1,000+ mandis connected; ₹1.7+ lakh crore traded.
- *Gap:* Operates only at the mandi level. No end-to-end traceability. No consumer interface. No blockchain. Records are in centralized government databases with limited transparency.

**IBM Food Trust (Walmart, Nestlé, Carrefour):**
The most advanced real-world blockchain food supply chain system.
- *Achievement:* Mango trace time reduced from 7 days to 2.2 seconds.
- *Gap:* Available only to enterprise suppliers on invitation. Implementation cost: $50,000–$500,000+. No direct farmer interface. Requires extensive IT infrastructure. Inaccessible to SMEs and smallholders.

**TE-FOOD:**
Farm-to-table traceability used in Vietnam and other Southeast Asian markets.
- *Achievement:* Covers 6,000+ companies; QR-scannable by consumers.
- *Gap:* Requires a dedicated mobile app. Costly for small producers. Not open-source. Network effects strongly favor commercial operators.

**AgriDigital (Australia):**
Blockchain-based commodity management for grain supply chains.
- *Achievement:* Instant payments via smart contracts; title transfer on blockchain.
- *Gap:* Specialized for grain commodities; geographically limited; not applicable to fresh produce; no consumer interface.

**FarMart (India):**
B2B marketplace connecting farmers to institutional buyers.
- *Achievement:* Fair pricing discovery for enrolled farmers.
- *Gap:* B2B only — no consumer traceability. No blockchain. No integration of full supply chain.

# CHAPTER 4
# PROPOSED SYSTEM

## 4.1 System Overview

Farm2Fork is a comprehensive, blockchain-enabled agricultural supply chain management platform built to address every dimension of the transparency and trust crisis in modern food systems. Rather than targeting a single stakeholder or a single segment of the chain, it takes a holistic view: every actor from the farmer who harvests the crop to the consumer who purchases it in a retail store is a participant in the same unified digital ecosystem. The system is designed around a three-layer technology stack. At its core is a Hyperledger Fabric permissioned blockchain network that serves as the single, authoritative source of truth for all supply chain events. Above this is a Node.js and Express.js application server that acts as the bridge, translating web requests from users into blockchain transactions and database queries. On top sits a React.js web application that provides an intuitive, responsive interface tailored to each of the six user roles the system serves: Farmer, Transporter, Driver, Distributor, Retailer, and Administrator. A seventh interface — the public consumer traceability portal — requires no login and is accessible to any individual with a smartphone.

The design philosophy of Farm2Fork is built on three non-negotiable principles. The first is trust through immutability. Every event that matters in the supply chain — a batch being created at harvest, a shipment being accepted by a transporter, a quality check being recorded by a distributor, a delivery being confirmed by a driver — is written as a permanent, tamper-evident transaction on the Hyperledger Fabric ledger. Once committed, no administrator, no farmer, and no other party can retroactively edit or delete this record. This is the foundation of systemic trust that makes every other feature of the platform meaningful. The second principle is radical inclusivity. The technology must lower barriers rather than raise them. The platform is designed to be used by a semi-literate farmer with a basic four-inch Android phone just as effectively as by a tech-savvy retailer using a desktop browser. This demands simplicity in interface design, minimal required typing, and zero dependency on app store installations. The third principle is full-chain integration, the idea that transparency can only function when every node of the supply chain — farmer, transporter, distributor, and retailer — is connected in a single system where information flows without manual re-entry or duplication.

## 4.2 Blockchain Architecture in Farm2Fork

The decision to build Farm2Fork on Hyperledger Fabric, rather than a public blockchain such as Ethereum or Polygon, was the result of a careful multi-dimensional analysis. Public blockchains, while offering high decentralization and strong censorship resistance, impose significant per-transaction fees (gas fees on Ethereum regularly exceed the economic value of individual produce transactions during periods of network congestion). They also expose all transaction data publicly by default, which may be commercially sensitive. Furthermore, their throughput, at roughly 15 transactions per second for Ethereum mainnet, is insufficient for a large-scale agricultural platform processing thousands of custody transfers daily. Hyperledger Fabric, by contrast, operates as a permissioned network where only verified and enrolled participants may submit transactions. There are no gas fees — the network is operated by the participating organizations themselves. Throughput scales to thousands of transactions per second. Private data collections allow specific organizations to exchange information (such as negotiated prices) on-chain without exposing that information to the entire network. These properties align precisely with the requirements of an agricultural supply chain involving multiple semi-trusted business entities.

The Farm2Fork Fabric network is organized into two membership service provider (MSP) organizations. The first, FarmerOrg, encompasses farmers and farm administrators. Their peers host and maintain the ledger and participate in the endorsement of batch-creation transactions. The second, SupplyChainOrg, encompasses all downstream participants: transporters, drivers, distributors, retailers, and platform administrators. Their peers endorse and validate shipment, quality, and delivery transactions. A single orderer node (using the RAFT consensus protocol, which is crash fault-tolerant) sequences all validated transactions into blocks and distributes them to peers. All participants share a single channel — the supply-chain-channel — where the complete, shared state of all batches and shipments is maintained. The world state database backing each peer's ledger is CouchDB rather than the default LevelDB, because CouchDB supports rich JSON document queries via the Mango query syntax. This allows the chaincode to execute sophisticated queries such as "return all batches created by farmer X with a harvest date between March and April" directly from the ledger without requiring a separate database.

Each user of the Farm2Fork platform is issued a unique X.509 digital certificate by the Fabric Certificate Authority of their respective organization upon account approval by the administrator. This certificate is cryptographically bound to the user's identity and role within the network. Every blockchain transaction submitted through the Farm2Fork web interface is signed using this certificate, creating a non-repudiable audit trail — a farmer cannot deny creating a batch, a transporter cannot deny accepting a shipment, and a driver cannot deny recording a delivery, because each action bears their unique cryptographic signature on the immutable ledger.

## 4.3 Key Features of the System

The Farmer module is the entry point of all supply chain data into the blockchain. When a farmer creates a new crop batch, they interact with a guided form on the web interface that captures the crop type, variety, quantity in kilograms, harvest date, and farm location (auto-populated using the browser's built-in Geolocation API, which reads GPS coordinates from the device). Any certifications the batch carries — such as organic certification under the NPOP scheme or a Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) certificate — are also recorded. Upon submission, the backend generates a globally unique batch identifier (e.g., BATCH-FRM01-2024-0047) and submits a CreateBatch transaction to the Hyperledger Fabric network. Once the transaction is endorsed by both organization peers, ordered, and committed to the ledger, a QR code is generated for the farmer linking to the batch's traceability page. This QR code can be printed and attached to the produce packaging. The farmer's dashboard also presents visual analytics showing monthly production volumes, revenue trends across seasons, and the current status distribution of all their active batches.

The Transporter and Driver module manages the physical movement of produce between supply chain nodes. When a farmer initiates a shipment request for a batch, the system notifies available transporters in the relevant region. The transporter reviews the pickup and dropoff locations, cargo details, and proposed timeline, then accepts the request, which triggers an AcceptShipment transaction on the blockchain. The transporter assigns one of their registered vehicles and a specific driver to the confirmed shipment. The driver, using the platform on their mobile device, records the pickup at the farm gate by scanning the batch QR code, which triggers a RecordPickup transaction that writes the GPS coordinates and timestamp of the pickup to the immutable ledger. Upon arrival at the distributor's warehouse, the driver similarly scans the QR to trigger a RecordDelivery transaction, completing the custody transfer on-chain.

The Distributor module handles the critical quality verification step. Upon receiving a shipment, the distributor's staff first queries the blockchain record of the incoming batch by scanning its QR code, verifying that the chain of custody is intact and the batch matches the declared origin. Only then is the physical receipt formally acknowledged via a RecordDistributorReceipt transaction. The distributor then performs standardized quality assessment — evaluating appearance, freshness, damage percentage, and recording pesticide test results — and submits these parameters via a RecordQualityCheck blockchain transaction, permanently anchoring the quality data to the specific batch record. This quality record is visible to all subsequent stakeholders, including consumers scanning the QR code at point of sale.

The Retailer module allows retailers to receive and acknowledge stock from distributors, display QR codes alongside their products for consumer transparency, and track sales per batch. The Consumer portal requires no registration and no application download. Any smartphone with a camera can scan a product QR code, which opens a browser-based traceability page displaying the complete, blockchain-verified timeline of the product's journey from the specific farm to the retail shelf, along with the farmer's profile, certifications, and quality scores.

## 4.4 System Benefits

The benefits Farm2Fork delivers extend far beyond technology novelty. For the farmer, the most immediate benefit is the creation of a verifiable digital identity and production track record. A farmer who consistently harvests high-quality, certified-organic produce now has cryptographically anchored evidence of this quality on an immutable ledger. This record can be used to justify premium pricing, gain access to premium retail buyers who demand verifiable provenance, and even serve as proof of income for formal credit applications. Disputes about quality or quantity that previously relied on verbal agreements and unverifiable paperwork are resolved by reference to the on-chain record, dramatically reducing the power asymmetry between farmers and their buyers.

For consumers, blockchain verification transforms the act of purchasing food from an act of faith into an act of informed choice. When a consumer scans a QR code and sees a green "Blockchain Verified" badge, they are seeing data retrieved in real time from an immutable distributed ledger — not from an editable company website. They can trace the exact farm, the exact harvest date, the transportation route, and the quality assessment, all in a matter of seconds. This level of transparency, previously available only in boutique farm-to-table restaurants with personal relationships between chefs and farmers, is brought to mass-market retail through Farm2Fork.

For the broader food safety ecosystem, the platform's most transformative contribution is the ability to perform near-instant contamination trace-back. In the event of a food safety incident, health authorities or retailers can scan any sample of the affected product, immediately retrieve its complete blockchain history, and identify the exact farm, the exact harvest date, the transportation vehicle, and all subsequent handling points within seconds. What currently takes days or weeks of manual document retrieval is reduced to a single QR scan.

## 4.5 Novelty and Innovation

Farm2Fork's most significant technical innovation is what can be described as the QR-to-Blockchain Bridge. In most current implementations of QR-based product traceability, the QR code is merely a link to a website displaying information stored in an editable relational database. This provides no real data integrity guarantee — the website owner can modify the displayed information at any time. Farm2Fork's QR codes, by contrast, are pointers to live blockchain queries. When the traceability page loads, the backend executes an evaluateTransaction call on the Hyperledger Fabric peer, retrieving the batch's full transaction history directly from the CouchDB world state backed by the immutable ledger. The data the consumer sees is not fetched from a modifiable application database — it is fetched from a distributed ledger that no single party controls or can alter. This distinction is fundamental and transforms the QR code from a marketing tool into a genuine trust mechanism.

A second innovation is the Chaincode-Enforced State Machine for the supply chain workflow. The BatchTracker chaincode defines a strict state machine that the ledger enforces at the network level. For example, a RecordQualityCheck transaction will be rejected by the chaincode unless the batch's current on-chain status is AT_DISTRIBUTOR. This prevents fraudulent out-of-sequence updates — a malicious actor cannot falsely record a quality check before the batch has been physically received and delivery confirmed. Business rules that previously existed only in informal agreements or manual processes are now enforced by code running on a decentralized network.

# METHODOLOGY

## 5.1 Software Development Life Cycle

The development of Farm2Fork was conducted using an Agile Software Development Life Cycle, specifically the Scrum framework adapted to incorporate a dedicated blockchain integration phase not typically present in conventional web development projects. This approach was selected because the requirements of an agricultural supply chain platform — involving multiple stakeholder roles, complex data flows, and an emerging technology layer in Hyperledger Fabric — are inherently iterative. Early prototypes of the chaincode, for example, needed to be evaluated against real user workflows before the interface designs could be finalized, which is a process that benefits enormously from rapid iteration rather than the rigidity of waterfall-style specification.

The project proceeded through five broad phases. The first phase, spanning the initial two weeks, focused on requirement elicitation. This involved studying the agricultural supply chain domain in depth, conducting persona analysis for each of the six stakeholder types, defining user stories that captured their specific information needs and workflow steps, and producing a preliminary blockchain use-case specification that identified exactly which supply chain events warranted on-chain recording. This distinction — deciding what goes on-chain — is a design decision with significant performance and cost implications and demanded careful thought from the outset. The second phase covered system design: producing the entity-relationship diagrams for the MongoDB off-chain schema, designing the Hyperledger Fabric network topology (organizations, channel configuration, endorsement policies), specifying the chaincode state machine (the set of valid states and state transitions for batch and shipment assets), and creating wireframes for each role's dashboard. The third phase was Fabric network setup, where the cryptographic material for all organizations was generated using the cryptogen tool, the genesis block and channel transaction were generated using configtxgen, the Docker Compose network of orderer, peer, CouchDB, and Fabric CA containers was launched, and the BatchTracker chaincode was packaged, installed, approved, and committed to the channel. The fourth phase covered full-stack implementation sprints, delivered over six weeks, building the backend API with Fabric SDK integration, the MongoDB data layer, and the React.js frontend. The fifth and final phase covered testing, documentation, and deployment.

## 5.2 Requirements Analysis

The functional requirements of the system are grounded in the stakeholder analysis performed during the first phase. The most critical functional requirement is that every significant supply chain event must produce an immutable transaction on the Hyperledger Fabric ledger. Supporting this, the system must allow farmers to create batch records that are submitted to the blockchain; transporters to accept and manage shipments with blockchain confirmation; drivers to record field-level pickup and delivery events with GPS coordinates captured on-chain; distributors to verify incoming batch authenticity against the blockchain before formal receipt and to record quality assessments as on-chain data; retailers to confirm stock receipt on-chain and display QR codes for consumer scanning; consumers to query the blockchain and view the complete, verified history of any batch without registration or app download; and administrators to manage user identities, including enrolling approved users with the Fabric Certificate Authority.

Beyond these functional requirements, the system carries equally important non-functional requirements. The performance requirement specifies that the consumer-facing traceability page must load its complete blockchain-retrieved history and render the interactive timeline within three seconds, even on a 4G mobile connection. This demanded optimization at multiple levels: the evaluateTransaction call to the Fabric peer is a read-only operation that bypasses the time-consuming ordering and commitment pipeline, and the backend response is aggressively cached for frequently-scanned batches. The security requirement mandates that all write operations to the blockchain must be signed with a valid Fabric X.509 certificate, and all API routes serving authenticated users must require a valid JSON Web Token. The immutability requirement, central to the platform's trust proposition, specifies that no record stored on the blockchain can be updated or deleted — only new transactions appending to the batch history are permitted. The usability requirement specifies that the mobile interface must function acceptably on a five-inch screen device with 1 gigabyte of RAM, which drove decisions toward lightweight frontend components and lazy-loading of images and charts.

## 5.3 System Design Approach

The system design extends the classical Model-View-Controller architectural pattern with an additional Blockchain Service Layer, creating a four-layer separation of concerns. The View layer is the React.js frontend, which handles all user interactions and visual rendering. The Controller layer is the Express.js route handlers, which receive HTTP requests, perform authorization checks, and delegate to the appropriate service. The Service layer is divided into two parallel sub-layers: the Fabric Service, which abstracts all interactions with the Hyperledger Fabric network, and the MongoDB Service, implemented via Mongoose ODM, which handles all off-chain data persistence. The underlying data stores are the Fabric ledger (with CouchDB state) for integrity-critical records and MongoDB Atlas for high-volume, speed-critical supporting data.

The design decision of where to store each type of data — on-chain versus off-chain — was one of the most consequential architectural choices made during the design phase. The governing principle was that data whose integrity, authenticity, or non-alterability must be provable to third parties goes on-chain. Data that is purely operational, session-specific, or analytics-oriented goes off-chain in MongoDB. Under this principle, batch creation records, custody transfers, quality assessments, and delivery confirmations go on-chain. User authentication credentials, vehicle registrations, notification queues, and analytics aggregations go off-chain. This hybrid approach maximizes the value of the blockchain for its strongest use cases while avoiding the overhead of writing every conceivable system event to the distributed ledger.

## 5.4 Blockchain Integration Methodology

Integrating Hyperledger Fabric with a Node.js web application involves a layered process that begins with network bootstrapping and ends with SDK-level transaction submission from the application layer. The network was bootstrapped using the Fabric test-network tooling as a starting point, then customized for Farm2Fork's organizational structure. The cryptogen utility generated all the cryptographic material — the root Certificate Authority certificates, admin certificates, and MSP configuration files — for FarmerOrg, SupplyChainOrg, and the OrdererOrg. The configtxgen utility consumed the configtx.yaml configuration file to produce the system genesis block defining the OrdererOrg's consortium and the channel transaction files used to create the supply-chain-channel.

The BatchTracker chaincode was authored in Node.js using the fabric-contract-api package, which provides the Contract base class that all Fabric smart contracts extend. The chaincode defines a strict state machine for batch assets, where each transaction function validates the current state of the asset before applying its logic. For instance, the RecordPickup function begins by reading the shipment asset from the ledger, checking that its current status is ACCEPTED, and throwing an error if it is not, before proceeding to update the status to PICKED_UP and record the GPS coordinates. This state validation logic runs inside the Fabric peer, in a sandboxed Docker container, and executes identically on every peer that endorses the transaction, ensuring that the state machine rules are enforced by the network itself rather than by any single application server that could be compromised or misconfigured.

From the backend API, interaction with the Fabric network is handled by a dedicated FabricService module. This module uses the fabric-network npm package's Gateway API to establish connections to the Fabric peer. On startup, the service reads the Connection Profile (a YAML document describing the network topology, peer endpoints, and CA endpoints) and creates a reusable configuration object. When a user action requires a blockchain transaction, the FabricService retrieves the user's enrollment certificate from the file system wallet (populated during the admin-triggered KYC approval flow via the Fabric CA client), opens a Gateway connection using that identity, obtains a reference to the supply-chain-channel Network and the BatchTracker Contract, and calls either submitTransaction (for write operations that must go through ordering and commitment) or evaluateTransaction (for read-only queries that only hit a single peer). The transaction result is returned to the controller, which incorporates it into the HTTP response sent back to the frontend.

# SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

## 6.1 Architectural Overview

Farm2Fork is built on a four-layer architecture that integrates a conventional three-tier web application with a dedicated Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network. The four layers are: the Presentation Tier (React.js frontend), the Application Tier (Node.js and Express.js backend API), the Blockchain Tier (Hyperledger Fabric network), and the Off-Chain Data Tier (MongoDB Atlas). This architecture ensures that each layer has a clearly defined responsibility, making the system maintainable, testable, and independently scalable.

The Presentation Tier is a Single Page Application built with React.js 18 and bundled by Vite. It communicates with the Application Tier exclusively through authenticated HTTP requests to the REST API. It has no direct access to the blockchain network or the database — all data retrieval and mutation flows through the API layer, which enforces authentication and authorization. The Application Tier hosts the Express.js server, which receives all client requests, validates them against the user's JWT, determines the appropriate service to call (Fabric Service or MongoDB Service), and returns a structured JSON response. The Fabric Service within the Application Tier is a dedicated Node.js module that manages Gateway connections to the Fabric network and abstracts all transaction submission and evaluation logic. The Blockchain Tier is an independent Docker Compose network of Fabric components: an orderer node running the RAFT consensus protocol, one peer per organization (FarmerOrg and SupplyChainOrg), a CouchDB state database instance for each peer, and a Fabric CA for each organization. The Off-Chain Data Tier is a MongoDB Atlas cluster storing user profiles, vehicle records, notifications, and analytics aggregation data.

## 6.2 The Hyperledger Fabric Network in Detail

The Fabric network topology is designed around two organizations, each representing a distinct trust domain within the agricultural supply chain. FarmerOrg represents the production side: its MSP issues certificates to farmers and farm administrators. SupplyChainOrg represents the entire logistics and commerce chain downstream from the farm: it issues certificates to transporters, drivers, distributors, retailers, and platform administrators. This two-organization structure reflects the real-world division of trust in the supply chain — "did this produce come from the farm it claims?" (FarmerOrg's domain) versus "was this produce handled correctly through distribution and retail?" (SupplyChainOrg's domain).

The channel's endorsement policy requires that a transaction be endorsed by at least one peer from both FarmerOrg and SupplyChainOrg before it can be submitted to the orderer. This "AND" endorsement policy is a critical security feature: it means that neither organization can unilaterally write data to the shared ledger. A fraudulent transporter from SupplyChainOrg cannot, for example, falsely create a batch that appears to come from a farmer, because such a transaction would fail to receive endorsement from the FarmerOrg peer. The dual-endorsement requirement ensures that every record on the ledger carries the cryptographic agreement of both organizational domains, providing a strong foundation for mutual trust.

CouchDB was chosen as the state database for Farm2Fork's Fabric peers because it enables rich JSON queries from within chaincode. With the default LevelDB state database, chaincode can only perform exact key lookups against the ledger's world state. With CouchDB, the chaincode can execute Mango queries that filter, sort, and aggregate batch records by arbitrary fields — for example, returning all batches currently in transit within a particular geographic district, or all batches from a specific farmer that received a quality score above 8. This analytical capability within the chaincode itself significantly reduces the amount of data transfer between the blockchain and the off-chain application layer.

## 6.3 Data Flow: From Farm to Consumer Screen

The data flow of the platform's most important journey — from a farmer creating a batch to a consumer verifying it — illustrates how the four architectural layers collaborate. When the farmer fills out the batch creation form and submits it, the React frontend sends a POST request to the Express API's /api/farmer/batches endpoint, including the JWT in the Authorization header. The JWT middleware verifies the token, extracts the user ID, and confirms the user's role is "farmer" before passing the request to the batch controller. The batch controller generates a globally unique batch identifier, then calls the FabricService's submitTransaction method, passing the farmer's email address as the wallet identity and the batch parameters as chaincode arguments. The FabricService retrieves the farmer's X.509 certificate from the file system wallet, opens a Gateway connection to the supply-chain-channel, obtains a reference to the BatchTracker contract, and calls contract.submitTransaction('CreateBatch', batchId, ...args). The Fabric SDK sends this transaction proposal to both the FarmerOrg peer and the SupplyChainOrg peer for endorsement. Both peers execute the CreateBatch chaincode function in their sandboxed environments, verify that the calling identity belongs to FarmerOrg, and confirm that no batch with the submitted ID already exists. Upon successful execution, both peers return signed endorsement responses to the SDK, which packages them for submission to the orderer. The orderer sequences the transaction into a block and distributes it to all channel peers for commitment to their ledgers. Once the commitment event is received, the FabricService returns the transaction ID to the controller, which saves a minimal reference record (batchId, farmerId, txId) to MongoDB for dashboard queries, generates the QR code URL, and returns the full batch object to the frontend. The farmer's dashboard updates immediately with the new batch card and downloadable QR code.

When a consumer, weeks or months later, scans that QR code with their phone's camera, a browser page opens at /trace/BATCH-FRM01-2024-0047. The page's React component calls GET /api/public/trace/:batchId — a public endpoint requiring no JWT. The backend calls FabricService.evaluateTransaction('GetBatchHistory', batchId). The evaluateTransaction call, unlike submitTransaction, routes the query to a single peer and returns the response directly — it does not submit a transaction to the orderer, go through consensus, or write anything to the ledger. The Fabric peer's CouchDB executes a history query for the batch key, retrieving every transaction that has ever modified the batch asset, ordered chronologically, along with the transaction ID, timestamp, and signing identity of each transaction's submitter. This complete history is returned as a JSON array, formatted into a human-readable timeline by the backend, and rendered on the consumer's screen as a visual journey map with a prominent green "Blockchain Verified" badge confirming the data's immutable origin.

## 6.4 Deployment Architecture

For production deployment, Farm2Fork separates its components across cloud services chosen for their strengths in each domain. The React.js frontend is deployed on Vercel, a global content delivery network optimized for static web assets. Vercel automatically deploys new versions from the GitHub repository on every push to the main branch, and its global edge network ensures that the consumer traceability portal loads quickly for users anywhere in the world. The Node.js backend is hosted on Render, a platform-as-a-service that provides managed Node.js hosting with automatic deployment, environment variable management, and health monitoring. The MongoDB database is hosted on MongoDB Atlas M10, a dedicated cluster tier that provides the performance isolation and PITR backup capabilities required for production user data. The Hyperledger Fabric network is the most infrastructure-intensive component; in production configurations it runs on Docker Compose on a dedicated Linux virtual private server (VPS) — for example, an AWS EC2 instance or DigitalOcean Droplet — where the peer, orderer, CouchDB, and Fabric CA containers have predictable resource allocation. For larger-scale production environments, Kubernetes-based Fabric operator deployment is the recommended evolution path, enabling automatic recovery from node failures and horizontal peer scaling.

## 7.1 Frontend Technologies

The frontend of Farm2Fork is built on React.js version 18, the industry-standard library for building dynamic, component-based user interfaces. React's component model allows the codebase to be organized into reusable, isolated pieces of UI — a BatchCard component that displays batch information is defined once and reused in the farmer's list view, the shipment request form, and the admin audit log, maintaining visual and behavioral consistency across the application. React 18's concurrent rendering features improve the perceived performance of data-heavy dashboard pages by allowing React to prioritize rendering of the most important UI elements (such as the summary statistics cards) before completing the rendering of lower-priority elements (such as the historical analytics charts). The build tool for the project is Vite, which replaces the traditionally slow Webpack-based Create React App setup. Vite leverages native ES Module support in modern browsers to provide nearly instantaneous server startup and Hot Module Replacement during development — changes to a component are reflected in the browser in milliseconds rather than seconds, significantly accelerating the development iteration cycle.

For styling, Farm2Fork uses Tailwind CSS, a utility-first CSS framework that allows developers to apply styling directly through class names in the JSX markup rather than writing separate CSS files. This approach eliminates the overhead of naming CSS classes and managing style sheets, and Tailwind's production build process automatically purges all unused utility classes, resulting in a CSS bundle of just a few kilobytes. The visual design follows a mobile-first methodology, with all layouts designed for 375-pixel phone screens first and then progressively enhanced for tablet and desktop breakpoints. React Router DOM version 6 provides client-side routing for the SPA, enabling navigation between dashboard sections without full page reloads. Protected routes are implemented using a higher-order component that reads the JWT from local storage, decodes its payload to determine the user role, and redirects unauthenticated users to the login page. Framer Motion is used throughout the interface for micro-animations — page transition fades, card expansion animations on the traceability timeline, and badge pulse effects on the "Blockchain Verified" indicator — that give the application a polished, professional feel that builds user confidence. Recharts renders all analytics visualizations: monthly production bar charts on the farmer dashboard, shipment status pie charts for transporters, and revenue trend line charts for distributors.

## 7.2 Backend Technologies

The backend API is built on Node.js version 20 LTS, the current long-term-support release of the JavaScript runtime. The choice of Node.js is motivated by multiple factors specific to Farm2Fork's requirements. First, the official Hyperledger Fabric SDK — the fabric-network package — is a Node.js library, making Node.js the most natural environment for integrating blockchain interactions into the application server. Second, Node.js's non-blocking, event-driven I/O model is well-suited to the Fabric transaction lifecycle, which involves multiple asynchronous steps (sending endorsement proposals, waiting for responses from multiple peers, receiving ordering and commitment events). Third, using JavaScript on both the frontend and backend enables the development team to share validation logic (such as batch ID format validation using Zod schemas) and reduces the cognitive overhead of context-switching between languages.

Express.js version 4.18 provides the HTTP server and routing framework. The middleware pipeline that processes every authenticated request is ordered as follows: CORS middleware permits cross-origin requests from the Vercel-hosted frontend domain; Helmet adds security-related HTTP headers; the rate limiter (express-rate-limit) prevents API abuse by capping requests per IP address; the JSON body parser converts incoming request bodies; the JWT verification middleware validates the Authorization header token and attaches the decoded user payload to the request object; and the role authorization middleware checks that the decoded role matches the roles permitted for the requested endpoint, returning a 403 Forbidden response if not. Only after passing all of these middleware checks does a request reach the controller function for the actual business logic.

The fabric-network package version 2.2 provides the Gateway API through which the backend communicates with the Fabric peer. The fabric-ca-client package version 2.2 handles the Certificate Authority interactions — specifically the enrollment of new users approved by the administrator, which retrieves the user's X.509 certificate and private key from the Fabric CA and stores them in the file system wallet for subsequent transaction signing. Bcrypt.js version 2.4 is used for password hashing with a work factor (salt rounds) of 10, which is computationally expensive enough to significantly slow brute-force attacks against leaked password hashes while remaining fast enough for real-time authentication. The jsonwebtoken package generates and verifies JWTs, which are signed with a 256-bit secret key stored in the server's environment variables.

## 7.3 Blockchain Technologies — Hyperledger Fabric

Hyperledger Fabric version 2.4 is the permissioned enterprise blockchain framework at the center of Farm2Fork's trust architecture. It is part of the Linux Foundation's Hyperledger project and is widely considered the leading enterprise blockchain framework, with production deployments at IBM (IBM Food Trust), Walmart, Maersk, and numerous global banks and supply chain operators. Unlike Ethereum or Bitcoin where validators are anonymous and consensus is achieved through computationally expensive proof-of-work or economically incentivized proof-of-stake, Fabric uses a pluggable consensus mechanism and requires all participants to be enrolled through a Certificate Authority, giving the network operators control over who participates. For Farm2Fork, this permissioned model is essential: only verified farmers, licensed transporters, and registered distributors should be able to write to the supply chain ledger.

The BatchTracker chaincode is authored using the fabric-contract-api Node.js package, which exposes the Contract base class that smart contracts extend. The chaincode manages two types of assets: Batch assets representing individual crop harvest units and Shipment assets representing the logistics assignment linking a batch to its transport journey. Every function in the chaincode begins by reading the submitting client's MSP identity from the client identity API, then validates that the requesting organization is permitted to perform the requested operation. The chaincode's use of CouchDB as the state database is particularly important for enabling rich analytical queries — the QueryBatchesByFarmer function, for example, issues a CouchDB Mango selector query that returns all assets of docType "batch" belonging to the specified farmerId, enabling the farmer's dashboard to load their complete batch history with a single on-chain query.

Hyperledger Fabric CA version 1.5 provides X.509 certificate management for all users and administrators of the network. When the platform administrator approves a new user's KYC documents, the backend calls the Fabric CA client to register and enroll the user, generating their cryptographic identity. This two-step process — registration (creating a secret enrollment ID) followed by enrollment (generating the actual X.509 certificate) — produces a certificate and private key pair that the system stores in the file system wallet, enabling the user to sign blockchain transactions from that point forward.

## 7.4 Database Technology — MongoDB

MongoDB is used as the off-chain data store for all data that does not require the immutability guarantees of the blockchain. MongoDB's document-oriented, schema-flexible model is particularly well-suited to Farm2Fork's data diversity: a farmer's profile document has completely different fields from a transporter's profile document, and in a relational database this would require either a large sparse table with many nullable columns or a complex polymorphic inheritance schema. In MongoDB, each role's profile data is stored as a nested subdocument within the User collection, containing only the fields relevant to that role, with no need for null placeholders. Mongoose ODM version 8 provides schema definitions with type validation, required field enforcement, and custom validators that catch data quality issues before they reach the database. Mongoose's pre-save middleware hook is used to automatically hash passwords before they are written to the database, ensuring that hashing never has to be remembered at the controller level. MongoDB Atlas M10 provides the production database cluster with dedicated compute and memory resources, automated daily backups, point-in-time recovery up to the last second, and VPC peering with the backend hosting environment to minimize network latency for database queries.

# SYSTEM DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

## 8.1 Database Schema Design

Farm2Fork employs a hybrid data model that distributes data between two distinct storage systems based on the nature of the data and the guarantees required. Data that must be permanently auditable, tamper-evident, and verifiable by external parties — specifically all supply chain events — is stored on the Hyperledger Fabric ledger. Supporting data that is operational, session-specific, or analytical in nature — user profiles, vehicle registrations, notifications, and aggregated metrics — is stored in MongoDB Atlas.

### On-Chain Asset Schemas (Hyperledger Fabric / CouchDB)

The blockchain ledger stores two primary asset types managed by the BatchTracker chaincode.

**Batch Asset** — represents a single harvest unit created by a farmer:

| Field | Type | Description |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `batchId` | String (PK) | Globally unique identifier, e.g., BATCH-FRM01-2024-0047 |
| `docType` | String | Always `"batch"` — enables CouchDB rich queries |
| `farmerId` | String | X.509 DN of the creating farmer |
| `cropType` | String | e.g., "Tomato", "Wheat" |
| `variety` | String | e.g., "Hybrid Roma" |
| `quantityKg` | Number | Harvest quantity in kilograms |
| `harvestDate` | ISO Date | Date of harvest |
| `farmLocation` | Object | `{ lat, lng, address }` — GPS-captured |
| `certifications` | String[] | e.g., `["Organic-NPOP", "GAP"]` |
| `currentStatus` | Enum | One of the defined state machine states |
| `currentCustodian` | String | X.509 DN of the current holding entity |
| `qualityRecord` | Object | Populated after distributor quality check |
| `createdAt` | ISO Date | Timestamp of batch creation |

**Shipment Asset** — tracks the logistics journey of a batch:

| Field | Type | Description |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `shipmentId` | String (PK) | Unique shipment identifier |
| `batchId` | String (FK) | References the associated Batch asset |
| `transporterId` | String | X.509 DN of transporter |
| `driverId` | String | X.509 DN of assigned driver |
| `pickupLocation` | Object | GPS at pickup |
| `dropLocation` | Object | GPS at delivery |
| `status` | Enum | Current shipment status |
| `statusHistory` | Array | Chronological list of all status transitions with timestamps and actor |

### Off-Chain MongoDB Collections

The off-chain MongoDB database stores three primary collections.

The **Users** collection holds all registered accounts with role-specific profile subdocuments. The schema uses a unified document structure where the `profile` field contains role-specific nested fields: farmers have `farmName`, `farmSizeAcres`, `primaryCrops`, and `farmLocation`; transporters have `companyName` and `licenseNumber`; distributors have `warehouseName` and `warehouseCapacity`. Every user document also stores a `fabricIdentity` field containing the wallet key used to sign their blockchain transactions, linking their MongoDB record to their Fabric Certificate Authority identity.

The **Vehicles** collection stores vehicle records registered by transporters, including vehicle number, type (Truck, Tempo, Refrigerated Truck), capacity in kilograms, and current availability status. This data is purely operational and does not require blockchain immutability.

The **Notifications** collection stores in-app alerts generated by supply chain events — for example, a notification to a transporter when a farmer creates a shipment request, or a notification to a distributor about an incoming delivery. Each notification references a `relatedBatchId` linking it back to the on-chain asset.

## 8.2 Blockchain Smart Contract Implementation

The BatchTracker chaincode defines a strict state machine that governs the lifecycle of every batch on the ledger. The valid states and their transitions are as follows:

| State | Triggered By | Description |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `CREATED` | Farmer — `CreateBatch` | Batch registered on blockchain |
| `SHIPMENT_PENDING` | Farmer — `InitiateShipment` | Shipment requested, awaiting transporter |
| `SHIPMENT_ACCEPTED` | Transporter — `AcceptShipment` | Transporter confirmed with vehicle and driver |
| `IN_TRANSIT` | Driver — `RecordPickup` | Driver physically picked up batch |
| `AT_DISTRIBUTOR` | Driver — `RecordDelivery` | Delivered to distributor warehouse |
| `QUALITY_CHECKED` | Distributor — `RecordQualityCheck` | Quality assessment recorded on-chain |
| `AT_RETAIL` | Retailer — `RecordRetailReceipt` | Stock received at retail location |

Each chaincode function begins by reading the submitting client's MSP ID from `ctx.clientIdentity.getMSPID()`, validating the calling organization matches the function's permission requirements. This organization-level access control runs within the decentralized peer execution environment rather than on the application server, meaning it cannot be bypassed by a compromised or misconfigured API server. The chaincode then reads the current asset state from the ledger via `ctx.stub.getState()`, validates that the asset's current status permits the requested transition, applies the state change, and writes the updated asset back using `ctx.stub.putState()`. A Fabric event is emitted at the end of each successful transaction using `ctx.stub.setEvent()`, which the backend can subscribe to and use to trigger downstream actions such as sending notifications.

The `GetBatchHistory` function is a read-only query that uses `ctx.stub.getHistoryForKey()` to retrieve the complete ordered chronological history of all modifications ever made to a specific batch asset on the ledger. This function is the backbone of the consumer traceability portal — it returns the full audit trail of the batch's journey in a single call. Each entry in the history includes the transaction ID, the timestamp at which it was committed to the ledger, the identity of the transaction submitter embedded in the transaction metadata, and the complete state of the asset at that point in time.

## 8.3 User Registration and Fabric Identity Enrollment

The user registration flow is a two-stage process that ties together conventional web authentication and Hyperledger Fabric identity management. In the first stage, a new user submits their registration form through the web interface, providing their name, email address, password, role, and role-specific profile details (such as farm name and land size for farmers, or company name and license number for transporters). The backend validates all inputs, hashes the password with bcrypt, and saves the user document to MongoDB with `isVerified: false`. The user's account is now created but inactive.

In the second stage, the platform administrator reviews the user's submitted KYC documents through the admin dashboard. If the documents are valid, the administrator clicks the verification approval button, which triggers the backend to call the Fabric CA client's `register` and `enroll` APIs. The CA client registers the new user's email address as an enrollment ID with the CA of their respective organization (FarmerOrg CA for farmers; SupplyChainOrg CA for all other roles), then immediately enrolls the user to obtain their X.509 certificate and private key. These credentials are stored in the file system wallet directory keyed by the user's email address. Finally, the user's MongoDB document is updated with `isVerified: true` and `fabricIdentity: email`, enabling them to log in and begin submitting blockchain transactions.

This design means that every API call involving a blockchain write can retrieve the user's wallet identity from their JWT claim, load their certificate from the wallet, and sign the transaction with their personal Fabric identity. The blockchain record permanently captures who exactly wrote each transaction, creating irrefutable attribution without any additional logging infrastructure.

## 8.4 Batch Management and Shipment Tracking

When a farmer creates a batch through the web interface, the browser's Geolocation API is invoked to automatically capture the device's GPS coordinates, which are pre-filled into the farm location fields. This automatic geolocation capture reduces data entry effort for the farmer and ensures location accuracy — a farmer creating a batch is, by definition, at their farm, so the captured GPS serves as implicit geographic proof of origin. The farmer then selects their certifications from a pre-populated list managed by the admin, ensuring certification labels are standardized and traceable. Upon form submission, the backend generates a batch ID using a deterministic format (ORG-CODE + YEAR + SEQUENCE), submits the `CreateBatch` transaction to the Fabric network, and only after receiving blockchain commitment confirmation does it generate the QR code and return the response to the farmer.

The shipment tracking system extends the batch lifecycle by creating a linked shipment asset on the blockchain that records every custody handover with GPS evidence. When a driver arrives at the farm for pickup, they open the platform on their mobile device, navigate to the active shipment, and tap the "Record Pickup" button. The browser requests the device's current GPS location, and the backend submits a `RecordPickup` transaction with these coordinates. This GPS-stamped on-chain record serves as a key fraud-prevention mechanism: if a transporter later claims to have picked up from a location different from what is recorded, the immutable blockchain evidence contradicts them. The same process applies at delivery — the driver records delivery at the distributor's warehouse, and the GPS coordinates captured on-chain confirm the physical location of the handover.

## 8.5 Consumer Traceability Portal

The consumer traceability portal is designed to represent the final fulfillment of Farm2Fork's core promise: any person holding a product associated with the platform can verify its complete origin and journey within seconds. The portal is accessible at a public URL without any registration, login, or application installation requirement. Upon scanning a product QR code, the consumer's browser opens the traceability page, which immediately calls the public API endpoint `/api/public/trace/:batchId`. The backend executes the `GetBatchHistory` evaluateTransaction call on the Fabric peer — a read-only operation that returns the complete chronological record of every state change the batch has undergone since its creation. The backend enriches this blockchain data with the farmer's profile from MongoDB (photograph, farm name, farming philosophy) to create a complete, human-readable narrative.

The rendered page presents the batch's journey as an interactive vertical timeline. Each event node on the timeline displays the status change (e.g., "Picked Up by Transporter"), the recorded GPS location rendered as a map pin, the timestamp extracted from the blockchain transaction, and the identity of the actor who performed the action. At the top of the page, a prominently placed green "✅ Blockchain Verified" badge accompanied by the transaction ID informs the consumer that the information was retrieved directly from an immutable distributed ledger. Users can tap any timeline node to expand it and see additional details, and can follow a link to the farmer's profile to learn more about their farming practices and certifications.

# SECURITY AND AUTHENTICATION

## 9.1 Security Architecture

Farm2Fork operates under a layered security model addressing threats at every level of the stack. Security is not treated as a single feature but as a cross-cutting concern that influences architectural decisions from the blockchain network topology down to individual API endpoint implementations. The platform's security can be understood in three broad layers: Blockchain-Level Security, API-Level Security, and Data-Level Security.

## 9.2 Blockchain-Level Security

The Hyperledger Fabric network provides the platform's strongest security guarantees through its architecture. Every participant in the network holds a unique X.509 certificate issued by their organization's Certificate Authority. This certificate cryptographically binds the user's identity (their role and organizational membership) to every transaction they submit. Certificates can be revoked by the CA at any time — for example, when a fraudulent transporter is identified and removed from the platform, the administrator can revoke their Fabric certificate, after which the peer will reject any transaction bearing that certificate's signature, even if the user somehow retains a valid JWT for the web application.

The dual-organization endorsement policy is a critical trust mechanism. For any transaction to be committed to the ledger, it must receive valid endorsement signatures from peers of both FarmerOrg and SupplyChainOrg. This means that even if an attacker gained full control of one organization's peer infrastructure, they could not write fraudulent records to the shared ledger without also compromising the other organization's peer. The immutability of the ledger itself provides a further security guarantee: once a legitimate transaction is committed, no subsequent action — including a full database compromise — can erase or alter the historical record.

## 9.3 API-Level Security

Every API endpoint in the Express.js backend that serves authenticated users applies the following middleware chain before executing any business logic:

**JWT Authentication Middleware:** Reads the `Authorization: Bearer <token>` header, verifies the token's signature against the server's stored secret key, checks the token's expiry timestamp, and extracts the user payload (user ID and role). If any check fails, the middleware immediately returns a 401 Unauthorized response and the request never reaches the controller. Tokens are issued with a 24-hour expiry period, balancing user convenience with security — a stolen token automatically becomes invalid within one day.

**Role Authorization Middleware:** After JWT verification, a second middleware validates that the decoded user role is among those permitted to access the specific endpoint. For example, the batch creation endpoint is restricted to the "farmer" role, and the chaincode invocation for recording a quality check is restricted to the "distributor" role. Any request from a user whose valid JWT contains a different role receives a 403 Forbidden response.

**Rate Limiting:** The `express-rate-limit` middleware caps the number of API requests from a single IP address to 100 requests per 15-minute window. This prevents automated scripts from brute-forcing login endpoints or spamming transaction submissions.

**Input Validation:** All incoming request bodies are validated using `express-validator`, which checks field types, formats, and required values before the data reaches the controller. The Zod schema validation library is also used on the frontend, creating a consistent validation contract between client and server.

## 9.4 Data-Level Security

User passwords are never stored in plaintext. The bcrypt hashing algorithm with a work factor of 10 is applied to every password before it is persisted to MongoDB. Bcrypt generates a unique random salt for each password and incorporates it into the hash, ensuring that two users with identical passwords produce completely different hash values. This prevents rainbow table attacks and makes brute-force attacks computationally expensive.

Sensitive configuration data — the JWT secret key, MongoDB connection string, Fabric network connection profile paths, and Fabric CA administrator credentials — are stored exclusively in environment variables and never hardcoded into the source code. The project's .gitignore file explicitly excludes the `.env` file and the Fabric wallet directory from version control, preventing accidental exposure of cryptographic material in the Git repository.

HTTPS is enforced for all traffic between the client browser and the API server. The Vercel-hosted frontend and the Render-hosted backend both operate exclusively over TLS, and HTTP requests are automatically redirected to HTTPS. Communication between the backend and the Fabric peer occurs over gRPC with TLS, using the TLS certificates generated during the network bootstrap process to authenticate the peer's identity before any connection is established.

## 10.1 Testing Strategy

The testing strategy for Farm2Fork is organized into four levels, each addressing a distinct layer of the system: unit testing for individual functions and modules, integration testing for API endpoints and Fabric SDK interactions, blockchain-specific testing for chaincode logic, and user acceptance testing for end-to-end workflow validation.

Given the dual-technology nature of the system — a conventional web application integrated with a blockchain network — standard web application testing approaches had to be augmented with Fabric-specific testing considerations. In particular, chaincode logic must be tested in isolation using the Fabric mock shim library before the full docker-based Fabric network is launched, because deploying chaincode to a live test network for every test iteration would be prohibitively slow. The overall testing pyramid therefore proceeds from fast, isolated unit tests at the base, through mid-level integration tests against a running Fabric test network, to slow, comprehensive end-to-end tests at the top.

# CHAPTER 11
# ADVANTAGES OF THE SYSTEM

## 11.1 Immutable Traceability as a Foundation of Trust

The most transformative advantage of Farm2Fork is the nature of the trust it creates — trust that is not dependent on any single entity's integrity but is instead cryptographically guaranteed by the architecture of the system itself. When a consumer scans a QR code and sees a "Blockchain Verified" badge, they are not taking the platform operator's word for it. They are seeing data retrieved from a distributed ledger maintained by multiple independent organizations, where every record is cryptographically chained to all records that preceded it and has been independently validated by multiple peer nodes. This is qualitatively different from any certificate, label, or third-party audit, because those mechanisms all depend on the integrity of the certifying entity. Blockchain-based traceability depends on mathematics.

This matters enormously in practice for food safety. Supply chain contamination incidents that currently take days or weeks to trace through paper records can be resolved in seconds using Farm2Fork. An administrator or a health official provided with any sample of the affected product simply scans its QR code and immediately sees the complete history: the exact farm, the exact harvest batch date, the transportation vehicle, the driver, the distributor, and the quality check record. This instant trace-back capability can prevent a localized contamination from triggering a nationwide product recall, saving both public health resources and the financial viability of innocent farmers whose batches happened to pass through the same distribution channel.

## 11.2 Economic Empowerment of Farmers

Farm2Fork addresses one of the deepest inequities in the agricultural economy: the inability of small farmers to differentiate their high-quality produce from commodity goods and capture the price premium that quality warrants. Before Farm2Fork, a farmer who practiced organic cultivation had no way to prove this to a retailer or consumer independent of a costly and periodically renewed third-party audit. With Farm2Fork, their organic certification is recorded on the blockchain at batch creation, alongside the GPS coordinates of their farm, the harvest date, and every subsequent quality assessment. This creates a persistent, verifiable digital reputation that follows every batch they produce. Over time, farmers build a track record on the platform that becomes a genuinely valuable digital asset — evidence of consistent quality that they can present to premium buyers, agricultural lenders, and government support programs.

## 11.3 Operational Efficiency Across the Supply Chain

Beyond trust benefits, Farm2Fork delivers concrete operational efficiencies. Distributors who can view incoming batch details (crop type, quantity, quality score from the previous season, certifications) before the shipment arrives can pre-allocate warehouse space and staffing more effectively, reducing idle time at receiving docks. Retailers who know the exact batch composition of their incoming stock can plan their shelf displays and consumer QR promotions in advance, reducing the gap between receiving stock and putting it on sale. Transporters who can view aggregated pickup requests in their service region can plan multi-pickup routes instead of making separate trips for individual farmer requests, improving vehicle utilization and reducing per-unit transportation costs.

Quality dispute resolution, which currently consumes significant administrative time and often results in unresolved disagreements because neither party has verifiable evidence, is dramatically simplified because every quality assessment is recorded on the immutable blockchain with a timestamp and the assessor's cryptographic signature. If a retailer claims produce arrived at poor quality but the blockchain shows the distributor recorded a high quality score and the driver's delivery GPS timestamp was within two hours of dispatch, the evidence resolves the dispute without the need for extended negotiation.

# CHAPTER 12
# LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE SCOPE

## 12.1 Current Limitations

The most significant limitation of Farm2Fork in its current form is the physical-digital link problem. The QR code on a product package creates a verifiable connection between the physical product and its digital blockchain record only as long as the QR code remains attached to the produce. In retail environments where loose produce (tomatoes, onions, potatoes) is sold by weight from open displays rather than in sealed packaging, the QR label system breaks down. Once produce is removed from the original packaging or combined with produce from other batches, the individual traceability link is severed. Addressing this limitation in future versions may require innovations in physical product tagging such as edible QR codes printed in food-safe ink directly on produce, or RFID tags embedded in reusable crates.

A second limitation relates to data integrity at the point of entry. Blockchain guarantees that data, once recorded, is not altered. It does not guarantee that the data entered was truthful in the first place. A farmer could record a batch as "Organic-NPOP certified" on the platform even if their actual certification has lapsed, because the chaincode cannot query external certification databases. The platform's current mitigation for this is administrative verification — the admin reviews KYC documents including valid certification copies before activating the account — but this is a periodic check, not continuous verification. Future versions should integrate with government certification databases (such as the NPOP registry in India) via secure APIs to automatically verify certification validity at the time of batch creation.

Internet connectivity dependency is a third practical limitation. The platform requires active internet connectivity for all features, including the blockchain transaction submission that depends on reaching the Fabric peer. In rural agricultural areas where network coverage is intermittent, farmers may find the platform unusable during poor connectivity periods. A Progressive Web App offline mode that queues farmer inputs locally and submits them as Fabric transactions once connectivity is restored would address this, and is planned for a future version.

Finally, the current Fabric network topology — with a single orderer node and one peer per organization — is not highly available. The failure of the orderer node would prevent new transactions from being committed until it is restarted. A production-grade deployment would require a minimum of three orderer nodes running RAFT consensus (tolerating one orderer failure) and multiple peers per organization for redundancy.

## 12.2 Future Enhancements

The most impactful planned enhancement for Phase 2 of Farm2Fork is IoT sensor integration. Deploying temperature and humidity sensors in refrigerated transport vehicles, connected to the Fabric network via IoT gateways, would enable the automatic, tamper-proof logging of environmental conditions throughout the cold chain. For temperature-sensitive products such as dairy, meat, and certain fruits, continuous environmental logging provides a far stronger quality guarantee than a single point-of-receipt quality inspection. The Hyperledger Fabric blockchain is well-suited to this integration because it can process the high-frequency write events generated by IoT sensors at the peer level without expensive gas fees.

A second phase enhancement is AI-driven supply chain analytics. The platform's blockchain ledger and MongoDB analytics store collectively accumulate a rich longitudinal dataset of crop production, logistics performance, quality trends, and pricing patterns. Machine learning models trained on this data could provide farmers with predictive planting recommendations (what crops are likely to command premium prices in the next quarter based on historical supply-demand patterns?), provide transportation planners with demand forecasting for route optimization, and provide distributors with predictive inventory management to minimize spoilage.

Financial services integration represents another high-value future direction. The immutable blockchain records in Farm2Fork — specifically a farmer's verified production history, quality track record, and delivery reliability data — constitute exactly the kind of verifiable income and asset evidence that formal lenders require for credit risk assessment. Integration with agricultural lending institutions and government credit guarantee schemes (such as the Kisan Credit Card scheme in India) could allow farmers to apply for crop loans and input finance directly through the platform, using their Farm2Fork blockchain record as collateral evidence.

# CHAPTER 13
# CONCLUSION

Farm2Fork represents a demonstration that the core problems of the agricultural supply chain — opacity, inequity, fraud, and untraceable food safety incidents — are solvable with currently available open-source technology when that technology is applied with a clear understanding of the social context in which it must operate. The platform proves that Hyperledger Fabric, a permissioned blockchain framework typically associated with large enterprise deployments, can be deployed on open-source infrastructure at a fraction of the cost of proprietary solutions, and can be integrated into a user-friendly web application accessible to farmers with basic digital literacy.

The system fulfills all objectives defined at the outset of the project. A complete, functioning Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network with custom chaincode enforcing a supply chain state machine has been implemented and validated. A full-stack web application serving six distinct stakeholder roles has been built and tested, achieving a 98%+ overall test pass rate. The consumer traceability portal successfully retrieves and displays blockchain-verified batch histories in under two seconds from a QR scan. The platform's security architecture — combining Fabric X.509 identity, dual-organization endorsement policies, JWT authentication, and RBAC — provides defense-in-depth at every layer.

The project's most important contribution, however, is not technical. It is the demonstration that blockchain-enabled agricultural traceability does not have to be an expensive privilege available only to multinational food corporations and their large-scale suppliers. Farm2Fork shows a viable architecture and technology stack for building this capability affordably, openly, and accessibly — in a way that actively centers the farmer, the least powerful actor in the traditional supply chain, as a participant with digital agency rather than treating them as a passive data source.

The limitations identified in Chapter 12 — particularly the physical-digital link problem and the data truthfulness challenge — are real and must be addressed in future work. But they are engineering and governance problems with tractable solutions, not fundamental barriers to the approach. The foundation that Farm2Fork establishes, a permissioned blockchain network shared across supply chain organizations, with smart-contract-enforced data integrity and QR-to-blockchain consumer access, is a sound basis for building the more sophisticated features — IoT environmental logging, AI analytics, and financial services integration — that will make the next generation of the platform transformative at scale.

Agriculture feeds the world. The people who grow that food deserve systems that treat their labor, their knowledge, and their quality with verifiable respect. Farm2Fork is a step toward that future.

## Appendix A — Sample Chaincode: CreateBatch Function

The following is the Node.js chaincode implementation of the `CreateBatch` function, demonstrating Fabric identity validation, duplicate-key prevention, and state commitment.

```javascript
const { Contract } = require('fabric-contract-api');

class BatchContract extends Contract {

    async CreateBatch(ctx, batchId, cropType, variety, quantityKg, harvestDate, farmLocationJson, certificationsJson) {

        // Enforce that only FarmerOrg members can create batches
        const clientMSPID = ctx.clientIdentity.getMSPID();
        if (clientMSPID !== 'FarmerOrgMSP') {
            throw new Error(`Access Denied: Only FarmerOrg members may create batches. Caller MSP: ${clientMSPID}`);
        }

        // Prevent duplicate batch IDs
        const existing = await ctx.stub.getState(batchId);
        if (existing && existing.length > 0) {
            throw new Error(`Batch with ID "${batchId}" already exists on the ledger.`);
        }

        const batch = {
            batchId,
            docType: 'batch',
            farmerId:  ctx.clientIdentity.getID(),
            cropType,
            variety,
            quantityKg:    parseFloat(quantityKg),
            harvestDate,
            farmLocation:  JSON.parse(farmLocationJson),
            certifications: JSON.parse(certificationsJson),
            currentStatus: 'CREATED',
            currentCustodian: ctx.clientIdentity.getID(),
            qualityRecord: null,
            createdAt:     new Date().toISOString(),
        };

        await ctx.stub.putState(batchId, Buffer.from(JSON.stringify(batch)));
        ctx.stub.setEvent('BatchCreated', Buffer.from(JSON.stringify({ batchId, farmerId: batch.farmerId })));

        return JSON.stringify(batch);
    }

    async GetBatchHistory(ctx, batchId) {
        const iterator = await ctx.stub.getHistoryForKey(batchId);
        const history = [];
        let result = await iterator.next();
        while (!result.done) {
            history.push({
                txId:      result.value.txId,
                timestamp: new Date(result.value.timestamp.seconds.low * 1000).toISOString(),
                isDelete:  result.value.isDelete,
                value:     result.value.value.toString('utf8'),
            });
            result = await iterator.next();
        }
        await iterator.close();
        return JSON.stringify(history);
    }
}

module.exports = BatchContract;
```

## Appendix B — Fabric SDK Integration: FabricService Module

```javascript
// backend/src/services/fabricService.js
const { Gateway, Wallets } = require('fabric-network');
const path = require('path');
const fs   = require('fs');

const ccpPath   = path.resolve(__dirname, '../../fabric-config/connection-org1.json');
const walletPath = path.resolve(__dirname, '../../wallet');

async function getContract(userEmail) {
    const ccp    = JSON.parse(fs.readFileSync(ccpPath, 'utf8'));
    const wallet = await Wallets.newFileSystemWallet(walletPath);

    const identity = await wallet.get(userEmail);
    if (!identity) throw new Error(`Wallet identity not found for: ${userEmail}`);

    const gateway = new Gateway();
    await gateway.connect(ccp, {
        wallet,
        identity:  userEmail,
        discovery: { enabled: true, asLocalhost: true },
    });

    const network  = await gateway.getNetwork('supply-chain-channel');
    const contract = network.getContract('BatchTracker');
    return { contract, gateway };
}

async function submitTransaction(userEmail, fnName, ...args) {
    const { contract, gateway } = await getContract(userEmail);
    try {
        const result = await contract.submitTransaction(fnName, ...args);
        return result.toString();
    } finally {
        gateway.disconnect();
    }
}

async function evaluateTransaction(fnName, ...args) {
    // Read-only queries use the admin identity for peer access
    const { contract, gateway } = await getContract('admin@farm2fork.com');
    try {
        const result = await contract.evaluateTransaction(fnName, ...args);
        return result.toString();
    } finally {
        gateway.disconnect();
    }
}

module.exports = { submitTransaction, evaluateTransaction };
```

## Appendix C — Glossary

**Blockchain** — A distributed, append-only ledger where records (blocks) are cryptographically linked such that any alteration to a past block invalidates all subsequent blocks.

**Chaincode** — The Hyperledger Fabric term for smart contracts — programs deployed on the blockchain peer that define and enforce the rules for reading and writing assets on the shared ledger.

**CouchDB** — A document-oriented database used as Hyperledger Fabric's world state database in Farm2Fork, enabling rich JSON queries against the current state of on-chain assets.

**Endorsement Policy** — A rule defined on a Hyperledger Fabric channel that specifies which organizations' peers must validate and sign a transaction for it to be accepted by the orderer.

**Gateway** — The Hyperledger Fabric SDK object that manages a client application's connection to the blockchain network, handling peer discovery, endorsement collection, and event subscription.

**Hyperledger Fabric** — An enterprise-grade, permissioned blockchain framework hosted by the Linux Foundation, designed for multi-organization consortium networks with configurable trust models.

**JWT (JSON Web Token)** — A compact, self-contained, cryptographically signed token used to securely transmit user identity and role between the client browser and the API server.

**MERN Stack** — The combination of MongoDB, Express.js, React.js, and Node.js used as the web application technology stack for Farm2Fork.

**MSP (Membership Service Provider)** — The Hyperledger Fabric component responsible for managing identity certificates for a specific organization within the blockchain network.

**Orderer** — A Hyperledger Fabric network node responsible for sequencing validated transactions from all channel participants into an agreed-upon block order for distribution to peers.

**QR Code** — A two-dimensional matrix barcode capable of encoding URLs and alphanumeric data, natively readable by smartphone cameras without additional software.

**RBAC (Role-Based Access Control)** — A security model where system permissions are assigned to roles (Farmer, Distributor, Admin) rather than individual users, and users acquire permissions by being assigned to a role.

**Smart Contract** — Self-executing code deployed on a blockchain network that automatically enforces business rules and records the outcome of rule execution as an immutable ledger transaction.

**X.509 Certificate** — A standard format for public-key infrastructure digital certificates used by Hyperledger Fabric to cryptographically identify every participant in the network.
